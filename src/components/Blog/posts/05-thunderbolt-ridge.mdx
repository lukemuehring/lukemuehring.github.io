This was the most challenging ridgeline I have done to date.
\
\
The lack of information, long approach, and remote location all contributed to the difficulty. The technical cruxes were both harder and more exposed than what I experienced on the North Ridge of the Pfeifferhorn, Geurt's Ridge and the West Slabs of Mt. Olympus, and South Ridge of Superior.
\
\
I first tried it in 2025. I made it all the way to the base when I heard the first booms of thunder. I chose to bail down Hogum fork, since I wanted to lose elevation rather than get back on the LCC ridgeline. It was quite the bushwhack to get back down.

<BlogPicture imageSrc={hogum_1} caption="Escaping down Hogum Fork" fullSize={true}/>

This year, I gave it another try, and I made a video documenting the experience [here](https://youtu.be/u_9g0kFQmEs).

## The Approach
I did the normal Pfeifferhorn hike to get to the ridgeline, then traversed across the south side of the Pfeiff and Upwop to get to the Hogum Bowl. 
<BlogPicture imageSrc={thunder_01} fullSize={true} caption={"Looking at the Pfeifferhorn after dropping south from the ridge."}/>

After traversing past Upwop, I went back up and over the ridge to enter a notch I had spotted on google earth beforehand, which allowed me to drop into the Hogum bowl.

<BlogPicture imageSrc={thunder_02} fullSize={true} caption={"First view of Thunderbolt ridge, going down and right in the photo."}/>
<BlogPicture imageSrc={thunder_04} fullSize={true} caption={"Looking down the notch at 40.52914034635002, -111.71603054251484."}/>

The top entrance of the notch was in a forest, and going down was a little bit loose and scrambly. I hugged the skier's right when descending. Both sides of the notch had many interesting rock formations.

Once through the notch, the hike ramped up in technical difficulty. The talus field guarding the base of the ridge is unpredicatably loose, and contains many large boulders that shouldn't seem to move, but still do. I had a little scare with some large talus shifting on me.

100 yards south from the base of the ridge, I found a bunch of metal scrap. I have read that this is actually from a plane crash back in 1936. The plane clipped the LCC ridgeline and ended up in Hogum bowl. There's a link at the bottom of this page that goes into the story.

Around 75% of the way up I started encountering 5.5 and 5.6 cruxes. I remember sometimes going climber's left to go around impassible sections on the proper ridge, with scary exposure below.
<BlogPicture imageSrc={thunder_39} fullSize={true} caption={"An exposed notch between spires."}/>

Then I got to the final crux, which was either 4 moves of 5.6 ledges (left) or a chimney (right). I opted for the ledges as the rock seemed solid, but the chimney looked more secure. I just don't have much experience chimneying, and I didn't want to start learning in that moment. 

<BlogPicture imageSrc={thunder_42} fullSize={true} caption={"Looking up at the final crux, with the chimney sticking out on the right."} />

After that, it wasn't too bad. The terrain for the last 100ft. was 3rd class and surprisingly better than the minefield of loose choss at the top of the North Ridge of the Pfeifferhorn. In fact, most of the climb on Thunderbolt ridge had solid rock, and I only felt a loose block once.

<BlogPicture imageSrc={thunder_48} fullSize={true} caption={"Summit blocks."}/>

Then, I had a lovely time making it back to the car, hitting the summits of Upwop and the Pfeifferhorn on the way back.
