<AdventureSection title="A season of scrambling" logline="The movement of climbing, on ridgelines instead.">
<PanelText>

So I spent a large part of last season scrambling and ridgelining instead - another way to enjoy the movement of climbing, without the technical difficulty.

</PanelText>
<PanelText>

On the bright side, it was a good experience:

- It's great conditioning for bigger objectives and adventures.
- It teaches you macro route finding and taking in the bigger setting.
- There's a feeling of having navigated a giant peak, with that awesome sense of scale and height, that you don't get from bouldering or shorter lines.

</PanelText>
</AdventureSection>

<AdventureSection title="Recovery" logline="Converge PT finds the real problem.">
<PanelText>

The folks at Converge PT saved the second half of my season. They diagnosed the problem as a muscle alignment issue rather than tendinitis and helped me actually recover. Within a couple of weeks of feeling good again, I was psyched to climb.

</PanelText>
</AdventureSection>

<AdventureSection title="Reflection Canyon" logline="Sixty miles of dirt road, ten echoes, pristine stars.">
<PanelText>

Before the Blue Steel arc, Chandler and I did a whole hiking, camping and ridgelining trip.

- Reflection Canyon felt like a long journey. The drive is 60 miles down the rugged Hole-in-the-Rock Road, and the hike in is long too.
- The echoes are the best I've ever heard in my life. Yell something and I swear you hear it 10 different times. It's quite special in that landscape.
- It's very, very remote, so you really feel like you're in the deep end. Beautiful stars, just pristine. Not much out there except the landscape.

</PanelText>
</AdventureSection>

<AdventureSection title="Day Canyon, Moab" logline="The hardest 5.9 hand crack ever, on my birthday.">
<PanelText>

After that, Chandler and I had a nice little adventure in Day Canyon in Moab.

</PanelText>
<PanelText>

- We were going to get on Pocket Rocket, Chandler's project. It's a big line with some of the coolest climbing features I've ever seen.
- We ended up warming up - or rather getting wasted - on the hardest 5.9 hand crack ever. It was one of my first 5.9 trad leads and quite the spicy test of my jams: even at 5.9, I was very uncomfortable and not used to what was going on.
- Chandler tried it later too. He was a good sport about it, although it may have tired him out for Pocket Rocket.

</PanelText>
<PanelText>

That's how I spent my birthday, actually. For the past two birthdays, that's what I've liked to do.

There's an Airbnb in Moab I really like and recommend. It's pretty decent for the price, the owners are really chill and friendly, and they have rental bikes in pretty good condition for exploring.

</PanelText>
</AdventureSection>

<AdventureSection title="Blue Steel" logline="Three sessions alone, worked on a rope, sent clean.">
<PanelText>

I projected Blue Steel solo. I had three sessions on the boulder this season, and I had it to myself every single time.

</PanelText>
<PanelText>

- I worked it on a rope, because I didn't want to spend any more body dollars than I had to.
- I'm psyched to say I executed that style of ascent the way I meant to: I never took an uncontrolled fall, so the send didn't cost me much.
- On the day of the send, my friends Chloe and Johnny, who I met up with in LCC, helped me shuttle pads from my car to the boulder. I still had to do another shuttle from my house by myself, since the Camry has limited capacity, to say the least.

</PanelText>
<PanelText>

The climbing itself went smoothly. After a year of building trad anchors and learning about protection and logistics, I was confident setting up a rope to work the problem and trusting that my gear was good.

A year ago I didn't know anything about trad, so it's cool to bring new skills back to an older style of adventure - in this case, bouldering.

</PanelText>
</AdventureSection>

<AdventureSection title="Trad and solo highballing" logline="The caffeinated style of climbing.">
<PanelText>

After Blue Steel, I kept following my curiosity about trad climbing and solo highballing. They're an interesting combo because they go hand in hand: you build a trad anchor to work a highball alone, which is close to top-rope soloing in both vibe and equipment.

</PanelText>
<PanelText>

The bonus is that once you're confident enough to send, you get a very freeing style of climbing - highball bouldering, which is closely related to soloing. It's an interesting knob to turn for boldness, risk and spice.

It changes the whole experience. It caffeinates it: your focus sharpens, the tunnel vision gets intense, and so do the moments. A lot of things just fall away to the danger, the focus and the performance.

</PanelText>
</AdventureSection>

<AdventureSection title="Hellbilly and the Zen Garden" logline="Chewing slowly on a project.">
<PanelText>

Before the Creek, I went back to Hellbilly in the Zen Garden and realized I really just enjoy practicing and being at the boulders. They have nice hangs, and I like sinking my teeth into the experience - almost to the point that I don't want to rush the send, because sending means less of the experience left to have.

</PanelText>
<PanelText>

So I chew slowly, even though I like biting off a lot. As a result I don't finish many things very often, but it sure is a good time. I let go of the final result and work sections like a musician playing a piece with my body as the instrument: sometimes at half tempo, sometimes at full tempo, sometimes from the beginning, sometimes from the top.

</PanelText>
</AdventureSection>

<AdventureSection title="Indian Creek" logline="Hand cracks with No-Approach Nate, and a Ghost Sparky flash.">
<PanelText>

I finally got to Indian Creek with No-Approach Nate, and we cut our teeth - or rather our knuckles - on 5.10 and 5.11 hand cracks.

I got to a point where I trusted my trad gear enough to really go for it and trust it to hold me if I fell. That's the weird space where you're actively at your limit and don't know whether you'll stick the move or fall. It's a fun place to play, but it's taxing, and hard to get to in just the right way.

</PanelText>
<PanelText>

When it worked, the result was a flash of Ghost Sparky, a 5.11+ at the Sparks Wall.

Funny enough, right after that flash I got very humbled at Supercrack Buttress trying its classic namesake. That was probably the first route where I realized there are genuinely levels to hand jamming, so I decided to focus on it more on the cracks at the BP.

</PanelText>
</AdventureSection>

<AdventureSection title="Airwolf" logline="A flash, and three pads hauled up alone.">
<PanelText>

I also flashed Airwolf, just a really interesting piece of stone that begs to be climbed, even though getting off it is kind of hard.

It was a solo session, so hauling three pads from the car up to the shelf it perches on was quite tough.

</PanelText>
</AdventureSection>

<AdventureSection title="The Zen Garden again" logline="Every move done, no send yet.">
<PanelText>

After the Creek and Airwolf, I did another session in the beautiful Zen Garden. I think it was my first time building a three-piece anchor to work the top.

</PanelText>
<PanelText>

- On that trip I officially did all the moves, even the crux move.
- With every move done, I can start to imagine the flow and the experience of doing the whole thing.
- I still couldn't send Hellbilly. It was too hard for me then, partly because it was too warm.
- I played mind games with myself about how badly I wanted the send: whether to force it by waking up super early, like 6 AM, and maybe send, or maybe not - who knows.

</PanelText>
</AdventureSection>

<AdventureSection title="Back home" logline="The Coffin, Bong Eater and new Micro Traxions.">
<PanelText>

After the Zen Garden I went back home.

- I started climbing at the Coffin and doing top-rope solo laps on Bong Eater.
- This is right around when I got the Micro Traxions.
- Then I went back to Lone Peak, the first of a lot of Lone Peak trips.

<AdventureSection title="Lone Peak" logline="A snowy season opener, and mentors on the granite.">
<PanelText>

The first trip, with Danny Dvorak, had the iciest and snowiest conditions of the season.

- The first pitch was basically a snow traverse. I had no crampons or tools, so it was a sketchy ballet - but one where we could use trad gear and somewhat protect it.
- It was one of the more interesting season-opener leads, at least for the Lone Peak season of late spring and early summer.

</PanelText>
<PanelText>

The next time I went back was with Liam Murphy, the Big Murph.

- Both of them - really all my trad partners - are more experienced in the art than I am, so they're all mentors to me. Every trip is a chance to pick up their skills and see how they like to do things. Everyone has their own preferred system.
- With Liam I did Vertical Smile and the Lowe Route on the Thumb.
- We meant to do one more, High Performance I think, but we had to end early.

</PanelText>
<PanelText>

I also got to try Interrogator on top-rope solo, on a fixed line. Man - some of those sequences are the best climbing I've experienced in a while.

</PanelText>
</AdventureSection>

<AdventureSection title="Squamish" logline="Trad multipitch with my brother, and the 5.13 behind Dreamcatcher.">
<PanelText>

After the Lone Peak arc, I went to Squamish for two weeks.

- I got on Dreamcatcher, and finally did some trad in Squamish - especially trad multipitch with my younger brother, which was awesome.
- I didn't do any bouldering this trip, which might actually be a first.
- I finally sent the 5.13 behind Dreamcatcher. It's a sick sequence, despite being really short.
- Squamish in the summertime is just so nice.

</PanelText>
</AdventureSection>

<AdventureSection title="Boulder and Rocky Mountain National Park" logline="Megaclassics, and the Flatirons' quieter lines.">
<PanelText>

Back in Salt Lake City, I headed out to explore Boulder and Rocky Mountain National Park for a bit.

- I did some hard bouldering and some megaclassics, like Hollow Llama and Tarzan.
- After Tarzan, I did more scrambling on the First Flatiron. I finally did a route other than the Freeway and got into the next-level, less-traveled scrambles: the First via Baker's Way, and Angel's Way. Angel's Way is sick.
